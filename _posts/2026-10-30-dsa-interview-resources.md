This list keeps only the classics. Each item has stayed in use for years, and each item has a clear job in your preparation. I group the items in the order that I suggest you use them: learn the basics, keep two references on the desk, switch to interview books, and then drill one problem list.

The list has eight items. You do not need all eight. Pick one item from each step.

![Four pale stone steps: a desk lamp and notebook on the lowest step, two thick reference books on the second, three bookmarked interview books on the third, and a desk calendar with check marks on the top step](/assets/blogposts/2026-10-30-dsa-interview-resources/01-four-steps.jpg)

## Step 1: A course or a textbook teaches you the basics

**MIT 6.006 Introduction to Algorithms (Spring 2020).** Erik Demaine, Jason Ku, and Justin Solomon [taught this course](#ref-mit-6006), and MIT OpenCourseWare publishes the lecture videos, notes, problem sets, and exams. Use it if you have not taken an algorithms course, or if your last course was long ago. Watch the lectures and do the problem sets, not only the videos.

**Algorithms, 4th Edition (Robert Sedgewick and Kevin Wayne).** The [booksite](#ref-algs4) links to two Princeton courses on Coursera, Algorithms Part I and Part II. The Coursera page says that [all features of the course are free](#ref-coursera-algs). Use this pair if you like to learn with working code: the book and the course implement each data structure in Java.

## Step 2: Two reference books stay on your desk

**Introduction to Algorithms, 4th Edition (Cormen, Leiserson, Rivest, and Stein).** People call this book CLRS. The MIT Press published the fourth edition [on April 5, 2022](#ref-clrs). Do not read CLRS from cover to cover for an interview. Look up a topic when a lecture or a problem leaves a gap, and read the proof there.

**The Algorithm Design Manual, 3rd Edition (Steven Skiena).** Springer published the [third edition](#ref-skiena) in 2020. The second part of the book is a catalog of common algorithmic problems, and the exercises include [job interview problems](#ref-skiena) from software companies. Use the catalog when you know the shape of a problem but not its name.

## Step 3: Three interview books teach you the interview

**Cracking the Coding Interview, 6th Edition (Gayle Laakmann McDowell).** The 6th edition has [189 interview questions](#ref-ctci), and each question has hints and a walk-through of the solution. Solve each question with the solution closed, and open the hints only when you are stuck.

**Elements of Programming Interviews (Adnan Aziz, Tsung-Hsien Lee, and Amit Prakash).** EPI comes in [three language editions](#ref-epi): C++, Java, and [Python](#ref-epi). Read EPI after CTCI, when you want more problems. Pick the edition in the language that you will use in the interview.

**剑指 Offer, 2nd Edition (He Haitao).** Publishing House of Electronics Industry published this Chinese book [in March 2017](#ref-jianzhi-offer). The book analyzes [80 typical coding interview questions](#ref-jianzhi-offer). Use it if you read Chinese and you interview with companies in China.

## Step 4: One problem list gives you daily practice

**Blind 75 (Yangshun Tay).** Yangshun Tay picked 75 important LeetCode questions and shared the list on Blind. Other people reposted the list, and the community [gave it the name Blind 75](#ref-blind75). Solve the list after Step 1, then go back to Step 2 and Step 3 for each topic where you fail.

This post covers coding interviews only. A sister post, "How to Prepare for Software Engineer System Design Interviews: The Resources", covers the system design interview.
