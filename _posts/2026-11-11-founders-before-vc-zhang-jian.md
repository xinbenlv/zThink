This post is part of the series "Founders Before VC". Each post looks at one founder who built a company before modern venture capital, which is usually dated to the founding of ARD in 1946. The question is the same every time: in an age with no venture funds, whose methods look most like a startup founder's today?

Zhang Jian (张謇, 1853–1926) is an unusual candidate. He was not a merchant. In 1894 he [passed the top level of the imperial civil service examination](#ref-britannica-zhang-jian), and he finished first, as *zhuangyuan* (状元), [in the 1894 examination](#ref-li-2017-p47). A year later he started to build a cotton mill. Most of his problems in the next four years were problems that a founder today would recognize: no money, partners who walked away, and a government that wanted a say.

## The market opened in 1895, and the money did not follow

Before 1895, a Chinese subject could not simply open a factory. Goetzmann and Köll describe a policy that [did not allow Chinese nationals to open private industrial enterprises independently](#ref-gk-2005-p159). Large industrial projects ran under the patronage of powerful officials such as Li Hongzhang.

The war with Japan changed this. The Treaty of Shimonoseki let foreigners [build factories in Chinese treaty ports](#ref-gk-2005-p159), so the court could no longer keep its own subjects out of industry. Cotton spinning shows the shift. Between 1890 and 1894 [only five spinning mills](#ref-gk-2005-p160) were set up, and all but one had government involvement. By 1916, thirty new mills were running, all under private merchant management.

So the market was open. What was missing was capital, and a way for private money to trust an industrial company. China had [no company law with required registration before 1904](#ref-gk-2005-p166). A large shareholder at Dasheng later said that the mill had been ["untouched by any law"](#ref-gk-2005-p166) in its early years.

## Zhang Jian failed to raise the money twice

According to the Jiangsu Provincial Archives, the founding records of the Dasheng mill cover the [whole process from planning and share raising in 1895 to start of production in 1899](#ref-jiangsu-archives-dasheng). The governor-general Zhang Zhidong [lent his support as patron](#ref-gk-2005-p165) and initiated the mill in 1895 as a joint official-merchant venture. Goetzmann and Köll add that he gave little else: he [was unable to offer Dasheng crucial financial support](#ref-gk-2005-p165).

The first plan was a merchant company with six directors, three from Nantong and three from Shanghai. Yao Zhenyu and He Xinyi reconstruct the sequence from Zhang's own chronicle and correspondence. By the autumn of Guangxu 22 (1896), [two of the six directors had already left](#ref-yao-he-2007-p25). The Shanghai partners kept reporting that they could not raise their share.

Then Zhang heard about a pile of idle machines. Officials told him that Zhang Zhidong had bought [a 40,000-spindle set of spinning machines that lay stored in Shanghai, close to ruin](#ref-yao-he-2007-p25), and that the government would hand them to merchants at a valuation of 500,000 taels. Zhang [proposed to use the official machines to hold the partners together](#ref-yao-he-2007-p25). The new plan was 500,000 taels of machines from the state plus 500,000 taels in merchant shares.

It failed again. The Shanghai partners later [said that 160,000 yuan of share money was fully raised but refused to hand it over](#ref-yao-he-2007-p26), and there was [talk of the shareholders breaking their agreement](#ref-yao-he-2007-p26). The solution was to cut the project in half. Zhang Jian and Sheng Xuanhuai each took half of the official machines, valued at 250,000 taels, and each would raise 250,000 taels in merchant shares for a separate mill. The contract said that the two gentlemen would [take the official machines jointly and each run one mill](#ref-yao-he-2007-p26). Yao and He call the resulting form *shen ling shang ban* (绅领商办): gentry receive the official assets, merchants run the business.

Zhang Jian later [told shareholders about these years himself](#ref-li-2017-p47), at the mill's first shareholder meeting in 1907. Li Yu reports that during the five years of preparation Zhang [never spent one coin of company money](#ref-li-2017-p50) on himself and lived on his salary as an academy teacher.

## The move: take an idle asset as equity, and keep control

In today's terms, Zhang Jian did three things.

**He turned a stranded asset into equity.** The machines were a sunk cost for the government. For Zhang they were half of his capital, with no cash needed. A founder today does something similar when a company contributes unused hardware, a dormant codebase, or a license in exchange for shares. The price was a government shareholder. The contract that Zhang signed with the provincial treasurer at the end of 1896 said that [hiring and finance were entirely in the hands of the merchant directors](#ref-yao-he-2007-p25), with one official director who could check the accounts.

**He sold a fixed return, not upside.** Like other companies founded after 1895, Dasheng paid [guaranteed interest at a fixed rate of 8 percent](#ref-gk-2005-p168) on shares. Goetzmann and Köll compare these shares to modern preferred shares. Zhang paid this interest even before the mill earned money. In the Chongming branch mill, by his own account, [the money for interest came from the share capital](#ref-li-2017-p52) before production started, and it came from borrowing after production started. Investors received predictable income. The company lost cash it needed for growth.

**He kept control without a majority.** In 1907 the capital stock of the No. 1 mill was [630,000 taels, held by 553 shareholders](#ref-gk-2005-p173). Accounts that can be linked to Zhang's family held 40,300 taels, about 6.4 percent. Zhang wrote the company charter himself in 1897, and the authors describe it as [highly autocratic and paternalistic](#ref-gk-2005-p174). The head of the central accounts office in Shanghai answered to Zhang alone.

The modern name for this package is a founder-controlled company with a strategic investor and preferred-style shares. The closest modern parallel is not a startup with a venture round. It is a public-private project in which a founder brings the operating plan, the state brings an idle asset, and small investors bring cash.

## The mill grew into a group, and the group bent under the founder's other goals

The mill [started production and made a profit in 1899](#ref-jiangsu-archives-dasheng). Its first shareholder meeting took place in 1907, when Dasheng became a [limited-liability stock company](#ref-gk-2005-p166). According to Zhang's 1907 report, the mill paid its fixed interest [every year, and dividends above the interest from 1900](#ref-li-2017-p52).

Zhang then built outward. In 1901 he issued the share prospectus for the [Tonghai Land Reclamation Company](#ref-li-2017-p49), which grew cotton for the mill. He explained the chain himself: the mill needs cotton, cotton needs farming, [so he set up the reclamation company](#ref-li-2017-p48), and an oil mill to use the cottonseed. In 1905 he funded the [Nantong Museum with Dasheng profits](#ref-cssn-museum), next to the normal school that he had also paid for.

Shareholders noticed. At the 1907 meeting, a prominent shareholder said that subsidies to the normal school [have nothing to do with the company](#ref-gk-2005-p173). He had no majority, and nothing changed.

The end of the story is debt. Goetzmann and Köll name several causes: transfers of company money to private accounts, risky loans to weak subsidiaries, falling yarn prices after the First World War, and too much debt from expansion. Together these [brought Dasheng close to bankruptcy in 1922](#ref-gk-2005-p175). A bank consortium took over in 1924, ordered the first external audit, and removed Zhang Jian as director. The historian Meng Sen, writing about the same period, put the cause in one sentence: Zhang [held both the company and the local government](#ref-li-2017-p52), so spending on Nantong became losses for the company.

## Three popular claims fail the source check

**"Zhang Jian was a purely private entrepreneur."** The sources do not support this. Half of the mill's starting capital was government machinery, and the share certificates from 1897 and 1903 said the mills were [to be jointly managed by officials and gentry](#ref-gk-2005-p165). Goetzmann and Köll found no document that formally ended this status.

**"Dasheng was the only private mill in China that made a profit."** Britannica says that the mill [became the only private mill in China operating at a profit](#ref-britannica-zhang-jian). Britannica cites no data for this, and I did not find a primary source that compares the profits of all Chinese mills. I treat the claim as unverified.

**"The banks took over in 1925."** Many Chinese accounts give 1925 for the creditor takeover. The chapter by Goetzmann and Köll, which uses the Nantong archives, gives [1924](#ref-gk-2005-p175). I could not check the archive myself, so this post uses the 1924 date and notes the difference.

Two more items I could not verify and therefore left out: the exact amount of merchant cash that was in the bank when the mill started in 1899, and the claim that the Nantong Museum was "China's first public museum". The museum claim appears in the [Study Times article](#ref-cssn-museum) that I cite for the founding year, but I found no primary source that settles it.

One number needs care. Goetzmann and Köll give the No. 1 mill's capital as [1.13 million taels between 1903 and 1914](#ref-gk-2005-p169), and its capital stock in 1907 as 630,000 taels. The difference is 500,000 taels, the same as the value of the official machines. The authors do not make this connection. It is my own reconciliation, and it needs the archive to confirm it.

## What a founder today can take from Zhang Jian

**An idle asset can be your seed round.** Zhang could not raise 500,000 taels in cash. He could take 250,000 taels of rusting machines. Look for assets that someone else has written off: hardware, data, a domain name that nobody uses, an abandoned open-source project. The owner may value a working company more than the asset.

**A fixed payout buys trust and costs flexibility.** The 8 percent interest made outside money comfortable in a country with no company law. It also forced Dasheng to borrow to pay investors. A revenue share or a guaranteed return can close a round today. Model what it costs in a bad year before you sign it.

**Control without accountability works until it does not.** Zhang ran the group with 6.4 percent of the shares. That control let him build schools and a museum. It also let money move between companies with no check. The shareholders could not stop it. The creditors finally did.

**Scope creep has a balance sheet.** Each new company had a reason: cotton, oil, shipping, education. Each reason was sound. The sum was a group that one person could not watch.

Zhang Jian looks like a founder because he worked with the market and the money that existed, not the ones he wanted. His story is also a warning. The structure that let him start later made the end hard.
