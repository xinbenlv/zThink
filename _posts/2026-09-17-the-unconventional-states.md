A community can exist long before it has a country. Its members may recognize one another, raise money, settle disputes, and organize journeys across borders while living under governments they did not choose. The interesting question is what happens when that community begins to govern parts of ordinary life.

Balaji Srinivasan gives this possibility a contemporary form in *The Network State*. His short definition describes an online community capable of collective action, crowdfunding territory, and eventually gaining diplomatic recognition. His longer account moves through a startup society, a network union, and a geographically dispersed network archipelago. Recognition is part of the destination, not something a property purchase automatically supplies. [The proposal](#ref-network-state) invites a historical question: how have communities acquired political power before, and what actually carried them across that distance?

There are plenty of suggestive examples. Christians corresponded across the Roman world before popes ruled territory. Merchants negotiated privileges abroad and sometimes fought kings. Religious settlers organized migration; national movements built schools and representative bodies before independence. Reformers bought land to demonstrate a different way of living.

But these examples lead to different places. An institution may become a territorial ruler, bargain successfully without becoming a state, or survive inside a larger state. Some experiments dissolve. Some acquire power over people who never joined them.

I find these histories most useful when we keep three questions separate: **Who belongs to the community? What can its institutions do? Over whom do they claim the right to rule?** Shared conviction can help answer the first question and motivate work on the second. It leaves the third unresolved.

## The church that became a territorial ruler

</figure>

### Letters before territory

Romans 16 offers a small, concrete view of an early Christian network. Paul recommends Phoebe of Cenchreae and asks his readers to receive and assist her. He greets Prisca and Aquila and the church meeting in their house. The passage connects named people, hospitality, and local gatherings across distance. It shows how an introduction could carry obligations from one community to another. [Romans 16](#ref-romans)

That is a useful starting point precisely because it is modest. A letter recommending a traveler is evidence of mutual recognition, not a constitution for a future state. Reading the later papacy backward into every early congregation would make the outcome seem intended from the beginning.

By the time Gregory the Great became pope in 590, the scale and character of church administration had changed. His first letter to the bishops of Sicily appointed a delegate responsible for the Roman church's patrimony there, called for regular episcopal meetings, and directed attention to poor and oppressed people. An organization that could delegate responsibility over distant estates had capabilities that a circle of correspondents did not. [Gregory's letter](#ref-gregory-letter)

Historian Carole Straw describes Gregory's papacy taking responsibility for food and water supplies, soldiers' pay, negotiations, estates, and charitable operations. These were material demands in a troubled city, not merely declarations of spiritual authority. [Straw, p. 5](#ref-straw)

The comparison with a network society becomes more useful here. Trust can help an organization collect resources and appoint agents. Administration then gives that trust a durable form: someone must keep accounts, distribute supplies, supervise property, and answer complaints. Belief does not perform those tasks by itself.

### A protector with an army

The next step depended on a changing balance of power. After the Lombard capture of Ravenna in 751, Pope Stephen II sought help from the Frankish king Pepin. Frankish campaigns in 754 and 756 forced concessions from the Lombards; the ensuing grants helped establish the territorial basis of papal rule. Paolo Delogu's account also warns against treating the grants as a perfectly preserved founding document: the scope of the 754 promise is disputed, and the donation instrument associated with 756 is lost. [Stephen II and Pepin](#ref-stephen)

This was a consequential political transformation, but a poor illustration of land following belief. The papacy needed an armed protector. Its territorial position emerged through war, negotiation, and competing claims to former imperial lands. A religious institution's capacity to administer property and a ruler's willingness to enforce a territorial settlement did different work.

The later history prevents an equally misleading conclusion: that the worldwide church and its Italian territory were the same institution. Italy annexed Rome in 1870, ending the remaining Papal States. The Lateran settlement created Vatican City in 1929. The Holy See, which represents the church diplomatically, is distinct from that territorial state. [Rome in 1870](#ref-papal-end); [the Lateran settlement](#ref-vatican)

A transnational institution can outlast the loss of most of its territory. Territorial sovereignty can serve its independence without encompassing its membership. That relationship is more complicated, and more interesting, than a community gradually coloring in a map.

## Two ways to organize the sea

</figure>

### The Hanse: power without a single state

The medieval Hanseatic League grew from merchants' need for protection and favorable conditions abroad. Town governments became increasingly involved, and assemblies brought representatives together. Its major trading establishments, or *Kontore*, included those in London, Bruges, Bergen, and Novgorod. [Hanse overview](#ref-hanse)

These establishments were not uniform miniature countries. Their arrangements depended on the host city and the privileges negotiated there. In Bruges, Hanseatic merchants stayed with local hosts rather than occupying a single enclosed compound. Elected officers defended merchants' interests; collective withdrawal could put pressure on a trading center. [The Kontore](#ref-kontore)

Law also traveled between towns. Lübeck's municipal law influenced other Baltic cities, but that circulation should not be confused with a sovereign legislature governing the entire league. A town's legal institutions, an overseas merchant establishment, and an alliance of cities operated at different levels. [Lübeck and urban law](#ref-lubeck)

The distinction matters when the Hanse appears on the battlefield. The coalition formed against Denmark in 1367 included both Hanseatic and non-Hanseatic towns, with princely allies alongside them. Its war led to the Treaty of Stralsund in 1370. City archivist Dirk Schleinert emphasizes the restoration of commercial privileges: the towns could obtain their objectives without overthrowing the Danish monarchy. [The Stralsund settlement](#ref-stralsund)

Here was a capacity for collective coercion and diplomacy without a corresponding project to turn all participating cities into one territorial state. It would be strange to call that an unfinished attempt at nation-building. For merchants who wanted access, security, and enforceable privileges, bargaining with rulers could be the desired outcome.

This makes the Hanse a valuable counterexample to any ladder that ends inevitably in sovereignty. A network might become effective enough to decide that it does not need a single government of its own. Its dependence on surrounding political authorities remains real, but so does its ability to influence them.

### Venice: a government extending its reach

Venice looks similar from a distance: ships, commercial connections, and scattered positions along the sea. Its political starting point was different. Pietro II Orseolo was already doge when he negotiated imperial privileges and led the Dalmatian expedition conventionally dated to 1000. An existing government was projecting power outward. [Orseolo's career](#ref-orseolo)

Nor can commercial expansion be separated neatly from conquest. In 1204, the Fourth Crusade sacked Constantinople, and Venetians shared in the spoils. The event belongs to the history of Venice's maritime power, however awkwardly it fits a picture of peaceful communities linking up. [The Fourth Crusade](#ref-crusade)

The later *Stato da Mar*, Venice's maritime dominion, required fortifications. UNESCO's account of its surviving sixteenth- and seventeenth-century defenses describes the protection of ports and routes from the Adriatic toward the Levant. These works sustained the republic's authority as well as its commerce. [Venetian defenses](#ref-venice-defenses)

The two histories suggest different questions for anyone proposing a dispersed polity. Is the project negotiating rights within several jurisdictions, as merchants often did? Or is a central government claiming authority over distant places? A map of connected coastal sites cannot answer that. Neither can the wealth moving between them. We have to ask who appoints officials, who can use force, and what position the inhabitants occupy in the arrangement.

## Belief, migration, and the people already there

  <figcaption>People seeking a new home encounter people who already live there. Conceptual illustration.</figcaption>

### Printed arguments meet territorial rulers

The Reformation offers a tempting analogy to the internet: ideas reproduced cheaply can reach people whom existing institutions cannot easily keep in line. Yet the printing press also served established authority. Cambridge University Library's exhibition of printed indulgences shows the same technology producing standardized religious documents that could be completed for individual buyers. Print increased the reach of competing institutions. [Printed indulgences](#ref-print)

The political settlement did not give every reader the freedom to choose a government. The Peace of Augsburg in 1555 protected Catholic and Lutheran imperial estates while excluding other confessions from that settlement. Its arrangements largely placed religious choice with territorial rulers. Section 24 permitted subjects of the two covered religions to emigrate with their families and sell their property, subject to obligations and payments. [Augsburg, §§15–17 and 24](#ref-augsburg)

That right of departure mattered. It also meant leaving a home and dealing with the costs of moving. Calling the result a religious “fork” would hide the difference between copying an argument and relocating a household. The spread of a belief, legal permission to practice it, and the practical ability to leave an intolerant ruler were separate achievements.

### The Latter-day Saints: making a place to live

The Latter-day Saint migration puts the organizational problem in physical form. The movement had suffered violent expulsion in Missouri, and Joseph Smith had been killed before Brigham Young led the 1847 migration west. The journey required preparation, route information, supplies, and coordinated labor. In the Salt Lake Valley, early arrivals were already preparing irrigation and crops when Young arrived in July. [The 1847 trek](#ref-mormon-trek)

An organized community could move people and begin building settlements. It could not settle the political status of the destination by doing so. The proposed State of Deseret did not receive congressional approval. Congress instead established Utah Territory in 1850 under federal authority; Utah became a U.S. state in 1896. In that last phrase, *state* means a member of a federation, not an independent sovereign country. [Utah's territorial history](#ref-utah)

The destination was also inhabited. Historian David Rich Lewis describes Ute and Shoshone use of the Salt Lake Valley and the conflicts that followed expanding settlement. Settlers' demands on land and resources, violence, and federal reservation policy constrained Indigenous life and contributed to dispossession. [Ute history](#ref-ute)

The migrants' search for refuge deserves to be understood on its own terms. So does the position of the people whose homeland became their refuge. Religious solidarity explains why settlers undertook the journey; it does not establish that everyone affected consented to the resulting order.

### The Yishuv: institutions before independence

The Jewish community in Palestine before Israeli independence, the Yishuv, provides a particularly strong example of institutions preceding a sovereign state. The United Nations Special Committee on Palestine reported in 1947 on an elected assembly and the Vaad Leumi, which administered education, health, and social services. It described the Jewish Agency's extensive departments and the Histadrut's role in organized economic and social life. [UNSCOP, chapter II, §§80–83](#ref-unscop)

These institutions were substantial, but the community was not politically unanimous. The same report recorded internal disagreements, the British Mandate's governing role, and Arab demands for independence. It also described the destruction and persecution facing European Jews. The urgency of refuge and the depth of institution-building belong in the account together with the conflicting claims of people living in Palestine. [UNSCOP, chapter II, §§81–96](#ref-unscop)

Israel declared independence in May 1948, following the United Nations' 1947 partition recommendation and amid armed conflict. U.S. recognition followed the declaration. [Declaration and recognition](#ref-israel)

That sequence cannot be narrated adequately as a well-organized community achieving its goal. Palestinian Arabs fled or were expelled on a vast scale during the war; the UN's historical overview describes more than half of the Palestinian Arab population being displaced. [War and displacement](#ref-palestine)

The history demonstrates that institutions can prepare a community for statehood. It also demonstrates why institutional preparedness cannot be the whole test of a political project. Schools, funds, and representative bodies can explain capacity. They cannot, by themselves, resolve competing rights to a homeland or justify what happens to those outside the founding community.

For a modern network-state proposal, this is a central problem. Membership can be voluntary while the exercise of territorial power affects nonmembers. Anyone evaluating such a project should look beyond its founding agreement to the position of neighbors, tenants, employees, children, and later arrivals.

## Living with the constitution

<figure>
  <img src="/assets/blogposts/the-unconventional-states/community.jpg" alt="Members of an imagined intentional community discuss work and resources around a shared table while one person leaves through an open doorway." width="1200" height="675" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  <figcaption>Institutions take shape through ordinary decisions about work, resources, disagreement, and departure. Conceptual illustration.</figcaption>
</figure>

Robert Owen's New Harmony and the communities inspired by Charles Fourier bring the question down to daily life. Their ambitions concerned how people would work and live together. They offer evidence about institutional experiments without needing to be recast as bids for diplomatic recognition.

In 1825, Owen bought an existing settlement at New Harmony, Indiana, from the religious community that had built it. His communal experiment soon fragmented. The National Park Service's account describes disagreements over rules and labor, successive reorganizations, and a breakdown within roughly two years. Educational work continued beyond the communal project's failure. [New Harmony](#ref-utopias)

The Fourier-inspired North American Phalanx, founded in 1843, lasted longer. It used a joint-stock arrangement, organized work through groups, and differentiated compensation rather than abolishing it. It dissolved in 1856 after financial difficulties that included losses from a fire. Different rules produced a different history, though neither community became a sovereign state. [North American Phalanx](#ref-utopias)

The short life of an experiment does not establish that its ideals were foolish, any more than longevity establishes that its government was just. It does force attention to matters a founding declaration can leave vague. Who will do unpleasant work? How are contributions valued? What happens when people disagree about whether someone is contributing enough? Can a departing member recover the resources needed to start elsewhere?

For an online community, these questions can remain distant while participation consists mainly of conversation. They become immediate when members share housing, depend on a common employer, or put savings into collective property. A formal right to leave is easier to exercise when livelihood, family ties, and capital do not all depend on staying.

This is where the history becomes most practical. Before asking whether outsiders will recognize a new sovereign, a community can demonstrate whether its own arrangements withstand disagreement. It can make accounts inspectable, define authority, provide a credible way to challenge decisions, and specify what members retain when they depart. None of these provisions guarantees success. They make the institution assessable while people still have choices about joining it.

### What the network can build

Srinivasan's own account acknowledges dependence on existing states: during much of the proposed development, physical law enforcement would remain the responsibility of the surrounding society. That qualification deserves as much attention as the eventual aspiration to recognition. [Law enforcement in the proposal](#ref-network-stages)

The histories above leave room for ambitious institution-building. A community can create services, organize mutual aid, coordinate investment, or negotiate useful autonomy. Those accomplishments may be worth pursuing even when independent statehood is neither available nor desirable.

I would judge an unconventional state by how its institutions handle disagreement and whether everyone subject to their power has a meaningful say. A community earns trust through the rules it lives by, including the rules that constrain its founders.
