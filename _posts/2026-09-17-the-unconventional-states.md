How far a community can go has always interested me, and I recently had a reason to look into the ones that went furthest.

A pope governing Italian towns, merchants sending fleets against a king, a trading company collecting taxes: political history has room for rulers who began with another occupation. Some organizations acquired territory almost as an extension of their everyday work. Others fought for it. Still others secured privileges and influence without becoming states at all.

How did those changes happen? A charter, a tax account, or a letter appointing an administrator answers that better than a map with the borders already drawn on it.

The cases below follow several routes: a church accumulating administrative responsibilities, cities coordinating trade and war, companies exercising powers granted by governments, communities migrating and building institutions, and reformers attempting to reorganize daily life. None of them is well described by asking when it became a state. The better question is which particular powers of government each one acquired, from whom, and at whose expense.

The chronology matters as much as the organizational form. Each case below begins with its setting and follows the change from early organization to mature power and, where relevant, decline or absorption. “State” remains a question to investigate: a merchant league, a colonial company, an independent republic, and a member of the United States were different political arrangements.

Wikipedia links provide background on named people, institutions, and events. Links on historical claims lead to the specific sources collected at the end.

## The church that became a territorial ruler

### Central Italy before the Papal States

The [Papal States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papal_States) developed in central Italy during the eighth century and survived, with interruptions, until 1870. Their geography joined Rome and its surrounding lands to territories across the Apennines toward Ravenna and the Adriatic. The starting point was a divided peninsula: [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire) officials still claimed imperial authority, while the [Lombards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lombards) threatened the remaining imperial territories. The church already owned estates, but [owning land and governing its inhabitants were different powers](#ref-papal-background).

### Letters before territory

Romans 16 offers a small, concrete view of an early Christian network. Paul [recommends Phoebe of Cenchreae](#ref-romans) and asks his readers to receive and assist her. He greets Prisca and Aquila and the church meeting in their house. Names, hospitality, and a house with a room big enough: that is the whole apparatus.

By the time [Gregory the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Gregory_I) became pope in 590, the scale and character of church administration had changed. His [first letter to the bishops of Sicily](#ref-gregory-letter) appointed a delegate responsible for the Roman church's patrimony there, called for regular episcopal meetings, and directed attention to poor and oppressed people.

Historian Carole Straw describes Gregory's papacy [taking responsibility for food and water supplies](#ref-straw), soldiers' pay, negotiations, estates, and charitable operations. Someone had to keep the accounts, distribute the supplies, supervise the property, and answer the complaints. Religious allegiance did not produce those people. Salaries and appointments did.

### A protector with an army

After the Lombard capture of Ravenna in 751, [Pope Stephen II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Stephen_II) sought help from the [Frankish king Pepin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pepin_the_Short). [Frankish campaigns in 754 and 756](#ref-stephen) forced concessions from the Lombards; the ensuing grants helped establish the territorial basis of papal rule. Paolo Delogu's account also warns against treating the grants as a perfectly preserved founding document: the scope of the 754 promise is disputed, and the donation instrument associated with 756 is lost.

Administering estates was something the papacy could do by itself. Holding territory took Pepin's army. The new protector also created a lasting problem: how could a pope rely on a powerful ruler for military security without becoming subordinate to him?

### The medieval struggle to make authority effective

The territorial grants did not create a uniformly obedient country. Roman aristocrats, urban governments, and provincial lords retained power; at times papal claims exceeded what officials could enforce. [Innocent III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Innocent_III) pursued territorial recoveries, and by the early fourteenth century the papal state had become [one of Italy's major territorial formations](#ref-papal-medieval).

The [Avignon papacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avignon_Papacy) exposed the difficulty of ruling Italian towns from a distant court. Cardinal [Gil de Albornoz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gil_%C3%81lvarez_Carrillo_de_Albornoz), dispatched in 1353, combined military intervention with administrative reconstruction. His [Aegidian Constitutions provided a lasting framework](#ref-papal-albornoz). The later [Western Schism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Schism), with rival claimants to the papacy, disrupted consolidation again. [Martin V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Martin_V) renewed the effort after his election in 1417. This was a recurring contest over appointments, obedience, and local privileges, rather than uninterrupted expansion.

### A Renaissance ruler with armies and alliances

By the later fifteenth century, the pope belonged to the [Italian balance of power](#ref-papal-medieval). But recognition as sovereign still left provincial dynasties entrenched. [Julius II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Julius_II) made that conflict concrete: in 1506 he [personally led a campaign against Perugia and Bologna](#ref-julius), bringing cities dominated by local ruling families back under papal control. Against Bologna's ruler he also used excommunication and an interdict. Military force and spiritual sanctions could operate in the same campaign.

His [alliances changed with the territorial objective](#ref-julius). He joined the coalition against Venice associated with the [League of Cambrai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_League_of_Cambrai), then helped organize a Holy League against France. A pope could negotiate with kings, field troops, and fight other Catholic powers. Religious leadership did not remove the rivalries of an Italian ruler.

### Beyond Italy: a papal line across the Atlantic

The papacy's reach was larger than its Italian territory. After [Christopher Columbus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Columbus) returned from his first Atlantic voyage, Spanish and Portuguese expansion raised competing claims to lands Europeans were only beginning to chart. In the bull [*Inter caetera*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inter_caetera), dated May 4, 1493, [Alexander VI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Alexander_VI) [set a pole-to-pole demarcation 100 leagues west of the Azores or Cape Verde islands](#ref-inter-caetera). The grant favored the Spanish monarchs' claims and belonged to an existing practice of papal authorization for overseas expansion.

That was extraordinary authority: a religious office in Rome could supply a framework by which European monarchs justified claims across an ocean. Yet the familiar story that “the pope divided the world” compresses two different acts. In the [Treaty of Tordesillas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Tordesillas), signed on June 7, 1494, the crowns themselves [agreed on a line 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde islands](#ref-tordesillas). Portugal claimed the eastern side and Castile the western side. The treaty also provided for a joint expedition to locate the line. Papal pronouncement supplied authority, but negotiation between the monarchies changed the settlement.

The westward shift [allowed Portugal to claim the coast of Brazil](#ref-brazil-line), leaving an enduring mark on the colonial geography of South America. It was not a survey of today's national borders. The treaty's own instructions for a [joint measurement expedition](#ref-tordesillas) show the distinction between agreeing to a line and establishing it on the ground.

Nor were the peoples whose lands were being assigned parties to this agreement. The treaty names European sovereigns as the recipients of lands already inhabited by others. Its importance for this essay is precisely that combination: authority powerful enough to shape imperial claims, exercised without the consent of the people who would bear their consequences. This was the papacy acting beyond the Papal States, rather than an extension of Italian provincial government.

### A more consolidated state, a less commanding European power

Later popes strengthened direct territorial rule. Ferrara passed under it in 1598 and Urbino in 1631. [Sixtus V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Sixtus_V) reorganized central administration in 1588; [Clement VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Clement_VIII) established the Congregation of Good Government in 1592 to [supervise the finances of subject communities](#ref-papal-administration). The machinery of rule extended into municipal accounts as well as diplomacy.

Papal government worked through cardinal legates, prelates, and subordinate governors. Central congregations handled executive and judicial business, while the Congregation of Good Government [reviewed revenues, taxation, and municipal disputes](#ref-papal-government). The practical powers were recognizable ones: appointing officials, supervising public money, deciding cases, and maintaining armed force. Local privileges complicated that hierarchy.

Stronger administration did not guarantee supremacy abroad. The [sack of Rome in 1527](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sack_of_Rome_%281527%29) exposed the state's vulnerability; subsequently it operated under the weight of [Spanish predominance in Italy](#ref-papal-administration). The history of the pope's spiritual authority and that of his territorial power did not rise and fall together.

### Revolution, restoration, and the loss of Rome

French intervention brought repeated rupture: a Roman Republic in 1798, Napoleonic annexation in 1809, and restoration after Napoleon's fall. In 1859–60 the papacy lost its northern and eastern provinces. The remaining territory around Rome depended on French protection; when that protection failed in 1870, [Italian forces took the city](#ref-papal-collapse). A state could endure for centuries and still depend on an ally at the decisive moment.

Italy [annexed Rome in 1870](#ref-papal-end), ending the remaining Papal States. The [Lateran settlement created Vatican City in 1929](#ref-vatican). [The Holy See](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_See), which represents the church diplomatically, is distinct from that territorial state.

The territorial state ended, but the Holy See continued to act internationally. The 1929 settlement secured a small territorial basis for its independence, without restoring its former Italian provinces. The papacy had acquired several kinds of power over its history; losing one did not extinguish them all.

### The Hanse: power without a single state

**Setting and beginnings: northern Europe, from the twelfth century.** The [Hanseatic League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanseatic_League) linked trading towns around the Baltic and North Sea, with Lübeck occupying a central position. Merchants operated across jurisdictions rather than inside one common country. Their [associations developed into cooperation among towns](#ref-hanse); there was no single founding declaration establishing a sovereign league.

The medieval Hanseatic League [grew from merchants' need for protection](#ref-hanse) and favorable conditions abroad. Town governments became increasingly involved, and assemblies brought representatives together. Its major trading establishments, or *Kontore*, included those in London, Bruges, Bergen, and Novgorod.

**Early institutions.** Their arrangements depended on the host city and the privileges negotiated there. In Bruges, Hanseatic merchants [stayed with local hosts](#ref-kontore) rather than occupying a single enclosed compound. Elected officers defended merchants' interests, and a collective withdrawal could cost a trading center its business.

Law traveled between towns as well. [Lübeck's municipal law influenced other Baltic cities](#ref-lubeck), though it spread city by city rather than through any league-wide legislature.

**Mature power.** The [coalition formed against Denmark in 1367](#ref-stralsund) included both Hanseatic and non-Hanseatic towns, with princely allies alongside them. Its war led to the Treaty of Stralsund in 1370. City archivist Dirk Schleinert emphasizes the restoration of commercial privileges: the towns could obtain their objectives without overthrowing the Danish monarchy.

They fought a king, won, and secured commercial concessions. This suggests a different measure of success from acquiring sovereignty over Denmark: the ability to make rulers respect merchants' access and privileges.

**Later decline.** The arrangement had limits. It had no regular central revenue, and participating towns did not consistently act together. Territorial governments and competing commercial interests narrowed its room for action in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. The [last historical Hanseatic assembly met in Lübeck in 1669](#ref-hanse-decline), although the league was never formally dissolved. Its institutions faded as their usefulness and the willingness to cooperate diminished. There was no capital to capture and no single central government to overthrow.

### Venice: a government extending its reach

**Setting before expansion: a lagoon at the northern end of the Adriatic.** The [Republic of Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Venice) grew from settlements on northeastern Italy's lagoon islands. UNESCO traces this development to [fifth-century populations seeking refuge from mainland raids](#ref-venice-origins). Its political formation was gradual, and its early ties to Byzantium connected it to an existing imperial world.

**Early development.** Byzantine emperors gave Venetian merchants [commercial privileges in the tenth and eleventh centuries](#ref-venice-trade). Venice looks similar from a distance: ships, commercial connections, and scattered positions along the sea. But [Pietro II Orseolo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pietro_II_Orseolo) was already doge when he negotiated imperial privileges and led the [Dalmatian expedition conventionally dated to 1000](#ref-orseolo). The Hanseatic towns sent merchants. Venice sent a head of state.

**Expansion and rule.** In 1204 the [Fourth Crusade sacked Constantinople](#ref-crusade), and Venetians shared in the spoils. That belongs on the ledger alongside the trade agreements.

The later *Stato da Mar*, Venice's maritime dominion, required fortifications. UNESCO's account of its [surviving sixteenth- and seventeenth-century defenses](#ref-venice-defenses) describes the protection of ports and routes from the Adriatic toward the Levant.

Venice also held mainland territory, the *Stato da Terra*. The later defensive system [protected both mainland approaches and maritime routes](#ref-venice-defenses). A trading city had become the center of a state with different territorial needs: ships needed ports, and those ports needed garrisons and sustained government.

**Later history.** Commercial relationships continued across religious boundaries, including with Mamluk Egypt and Ottoman territories. The republic's history therefore cannot be reduced to Christian fleets fighting Muslim ones. Its long independent existence [ended with Napoleon's conquest in 1797](#ref-venice-trade). Trade, colonial rule, and diplomacy had sustained the same government for centuries, but did not insulate it from European war.

Two maps of ports can look alike and tell you nothing until you know who appointed the officials, who could use force, and whose authority the inhabitants woke up under.

### Britain's East India Company: from trading posts to tax revenue

**Setting: an English venture entering established Asian markets.** The [East India Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_India_Company) initially sought the spices of Southeast Asia. Indian textiles and, later, goods from China became major parts of its business. Its agents [negotiated with local merchants for supplies](#ref-eic-origins); a monopoly issued in London did not itself make them rulers of the societies where they traded.

**Early organization, from 1600.** The English East India Company began with a [royal charter in 1600](#ref-eic-origins) granting a monopoly over English trade with a vast part of Asia. Its overseas “factories” were trading establishments: factors stayed behind to arrange purchases and sales while ships made the long return voyage. Directors elected by shareholders in London sent instructions to councils overseas. The original business was moving goods between markets.

**The military and fiscal turn, 1740s–1765.** British-French warfare spread to India in the mid-1740s, accelerating the company's [transformation into a military power](#ref-eic-origins). Before the [Battle of Plassey in 1757](#ref-eic-plassey), [Robert Clive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Clive) and the [Jagat Seth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jagat_Seth) banking family arranged to support [Mir Jafar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mir_Jafar) as nawab if [Siraj-ud-Daulah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siraj_ud-Daulah) were defeated. Clive's force contained about 2,100 Indian sepoys out of roughly 3,000 men. The company took Bengal with Indian soldiers, Indian money, and an Indian claimant to the throne.

In 1765 the [Mughal emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_emperors) [granted the company the diwani](#ref-eic-origins), the right to collect revenues in Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa. Taxes could now support troops and further expansion. The company had acquired claims on agricultural revenue as well as profits from trade. Its authority over a taxpayer no longer resembled its relationship with a customer. The later eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries brought [further territorial acquisitions](#ref-eic-origins), extending the revenues and responsibilities attached to company government.

**Mature government and outside control.** None of this went unnoticed at home. After the company's financial crisis, [Parliament intervened in 1773](#ref-eic-parliament) with a loan and a regulating act. The 1784 India Act created a government Board of Control to supervise its Indian affairs.

**Later history, 1833–1874.** The company [stopped trading in 1833](#ref-eic-crown) and went on administering territory. Following the rebellion of 1857, Parliament transferred its administrative powers, possessions, and armed forces to the Crown in 1858. The corporation survived until its formal dissolution in 1874. A business could cease doing business before it ceased governing.

### The Dutch VOC: powers written into the charter

**Setting and formation: the Netherlands and Asian sea routes, 1602.** The [Dutch East India Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_East_India_Company), or VOC, united previously competing Dutch ventures into a chartered enterprise. It entered a commercial world with existing Asian merchants and rulers, as well as European competitors. Its [formation from earlier companies](#ref-voc-transfer) made it possible to coordinate voyages and exercise its chartered privileges on a larger scale.

**Early powers and headquarters.** The VOC combined commerce with governmental powers from its foundation in 1602. The [States General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_General_of_the_Netherlands) [authorized treaties, forts, troops, and war](#ref-voc-charter), as well as the administration of justice. These powers accompanied an exclusive Dutch trading privilege across a huge overseas region.

The VOC established its Asian headquarters at [Batavia in 1619](#ref-voc-batavia), on the site of Jakarta after its destruction. The company dealt with Asian rulers and trading communities through negotiation, competition, and force; its authority varied from place to place.

**Expansion through coercion.** Islanders in the [Banda Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banda_Islands) traded spices with several buyers and resisted the company's demands. In [Jan Pieterszoon Coen's campaign of 1621](#ref-voc-banda), the VOC used mass killing, enslavement, and forced removal to impose its control over the nutmeg trade. The Dutch National Archives presents Coen's own correspondence alongside its account of the devastation.

**Later history.** The company outlasted its first conquests by nearly two centuries. Competition, war, and declining profits weakened it. In 1799 it was dissolved; its [possessions and debts passed to the Batavian Republic](#ref-voc-transfer). The [end of the company did not end colonial rule](#ref-voc-lifecycle). What changed was the organization through which that rule was exercised.

Both companies had shareholders and directors, yet exercised powers reaching far beyond buying and selling. Their charters, conquests, and later government takeovers show how corporate and state authority became intertwined. The charter is the document worth reading, because it names who handed each of those powers over.

### Printed arguments meet territorial rulers

**Setting: the sixteenth-century Holy Roman Empire.** The [Reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reformation) unfolded among existing principalities, bishoprics, and cities. These were not new states founded by printers or congregations. The [Peace of Augsburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_of_Augsburg) would become a settlement among imperial authorities about which religious changes they would recognize.

The Reformation spread arguments about religious authority across political borders. Print was part of that history, but it also served established institutions. Cambridge University Library's exhibition of [printed indulgences](#ref-print) shows standardized religious documents with blanks that could be completed for individual buyers. Printing carried Luther's objections and mass-produced the documents he objected to.

The [Peace of Augsburg in 1555](#ref-augsburg) protected Catholic and Lutheran imperial estates while excluding other confessions from that settlement. Its arrangements largely placed religious choice with territorial rulers. Section 24 permitted subjects of the two covered religions to emigrate with their families and sell their property, subject to obligations and payments.

A right to leave is a real right. It is also an instruction to sell the farm. The institutional outcome in 1555 was a regulated division within an existing empire, with territorial rulers strengthened in religious matters. It explains one pressure behind migration without making every later migrating community an extension of the Reformation.

### The Latter-day Saints: making a place to live

**Setting: from the American Midwest to the Great Basin in the 1840s.** The [Latter-day Saints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latter_Day_Saint_movement) brought a religious community and a history of forced movement with them. Their immediate departure point was [Nauvoo, Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nauvoo%2C_Illinois): the first wagons crossed the Mississippi in February 1846. The route ran through Iowa and Winter Quarters on the Missouri before [the 1847 journey to the Salt Lake Valley](#ref-mormon-timeline).

**Early settlement.** The movement had suffered violent expulsion in Missouri, and [Joseph Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Smith) had been killed before [Brigham Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brigham_Young) led the [1847 migration west](#ref-mormon-trek). The journey required preparation, route information, supplies, and coordinated labor. In the Salt Lake Valley, early arrivals were already preparing irrigation and crops when Young arrived in July.

**From settlement to territorial government.** Moving thousands of people did not settle what the destination would be. The proposed [State of Deseret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_of_Deseret) did not receive congressional approval. Congress instead [established Utah Territory in 1850](#ref-utah) under federal authority; Utah became a U.S. state in 1896. In that last phrase, *state* means a member of a federation, not an independent sovereign country.

Brigham Young became the first territorial governor. Church leaders also influenced political nominations, giving religious organization considerable practical weight within a territory that remained subject to the United States. Federal objections centered on [church political power and plural marriage](#ref-utah-statehood), not merely the logistical question of whether settlers could build towns.

The destination was also inhabited. Historian David Rich Lewis describes [Ute and Shoshone use of the Salt Lake Valley](#ref-ute) and the conflicts that followed expanding settlement. Settlers' demands on land and resources, violence, and federal reservation policy constrained Indigenous life and contributed to dispossession.

**Later accommodation.** Congressional pressure and antipolygamy legislation constrained the church's room for autonomy. [Wilford Woodruff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilford_Woodruff)'s 1890 Manifesto changed its official position on plural marriage; the constitutional process that followed culminated in [Utah's admission on January 4, 1896](#ref-utah-statehood). The political destination was participation in a federation. The community had built governing capacity, but the terms on which it could exercise that capacity were contested by a larger state.

For Indigenous inhabitants, meanwhile, settler institution-building also meant pressure on their own land and ways of life. Both experiences belong in the same history.

### The Yishuv: institutions before independence

**Setting: Palestine under Ottoman and then British rule.** The [Yishuv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yishuv) means the Jewish community in Palestine before Israeli independence. Its later institutions developed in a territory with an existing Arab population and competing national claims. The British Mandate incorporated the 1917 [Balfour Declaration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balfour_Declaration)'s support for a Jewish national home; the interwar years brought [large-scale Jewish immigration, intensified by Nazi persecution](#ref-palestine-background).

**Institution-building before independence.** By the 1940s the Yishuv possessed substantial governing capacity, though British authorities still governed Palestine. The United Nations Special Committee on Palestine reported in 1947 on [an elected assembly and the Vaad Leumi](#ref-unscop), which administered education, health, and social services. It described the [Jewish Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_Agency_for_Israel)'s extensive departments and the [Histadrut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Histadrut)'s role in organized economic and social life.

The same report recorded internal disagreements, the [British Mandate's governing role and Arab demands for independence](#ref-unscop). It also described the destruction and persecution facing European Jews.

**The transition in 1947–1948.** Israel [declared independence in May 1948](#ref-israel), following the United Nations' 1947 partition recommendation and amid armed conflict. U.S. recognition followed the declaration.

Palestinian Arabs [fled or were expelled on a vast scale](#ref-palestine) during the war; the UN's historical overview describes more than half of the Palestinian Arab population being displaced.

**The immediate aftermath.** The war left a territorial settlement different from the UN proposal: Israel expanded beyond the proposed Jewish state's boundaries, while Jordan and Egypt controlled the remaining areas assigned to the proposed Arab state. That [Arab state did not emerge from the 1948 settlement](#ref-palestine). For this comparison, the decisive transition is from communal institutions under a mandate to independent state power amid war and displacement; it is not a claim that the declaration settled the conflict.

The institutions help explain the capacity available when independence was declared. They do not settle who had a right to the ground it was assembled on.

## Living with the constitution

### New Harmony: an industrial reformer's experiment, 1825–1827

**Setting and predecessors.** [New Harmony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Harmony%2C_Indiana) stood on the Wabash River in southwestern Indiana. It was already a communal settlement before [Robert Owen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Owen) arrived. The [Harmony Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmony_Society), German religious separatists led by George Rapp, had [built its community there from 1814](#ref-new-harmony-background). Owen bought an existing town and its productive infrastructure in 1825.

**Early ambitions.** Owen approached the experiment as an industrial reformer concerned with class and education. With [William Maclure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Maclure), he [brought educators and scientists to the settlement](#ref-new-harmony-background). Buying buildings made an immediate start possible; it did not establish agreement among the people who would occupy them.

**Reorganization and aftermath.** The experiment depended on its backers' money and suffered disputes over work, social authority, and rules. Members began splitting away in 1826, and the communal undertaking broke down within roughly two years. [Educational work survived its collapse](#ref-utopias). The town's later life should therefore be separated from the short-lived attempt to organize all its inhabitants communally.

### The North American Phalanx: a longer cooperative experiment, 1843–1856

**Setting and formation.** The [North American Phalanx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_Phalanx) was founded in Monmouth County, New Jersey, by an association inspired by [Charles Fourier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Fourier). The first families arrived in 1843 to [prepare the property for the larger group](#ref-phalanx). Unlike Owen's purchase of a built town, this was a staged establishment on newly acquired land.

**Working institutions.** It used joint-stock ownership, organized agricultural labor into groups, and paid more for work judged important but unpleasant. Families had separate apartments and shared meals. Its [internal organization still operated within the United States](#ref-phalanx), using money and a business form recognized by the surrounding society.

**Dissolution.** Financial difficulties, including fire losses, preceded dissolution in 1856. The community voted to sell property, settle debts, and distribute the remainder. Its longer life rewards a close look at these practical arrangements, though duration alone cannot tell us which ones mattered most.

These two experiments leave me with four practical questions. Who does the unpleasant work? How are contributions valued? What happens when people disagree about whether someone is contributing enough? Can a member who leaves take anything with them? A statement of shared ideals still needs workable answers to each.

These were small communities, and their problems were the ordinary ones: land had to be paid for, work divided, disagreements settled.

## Different destinations

Ask when each of these became a state and most of them give no answer. The Hanse exercised collective power without becoming a single sovereign state. The East India Company did it in pieces, across a century, and was still a corporation when it stopped. The papacy did it, lost it, and got a fragment back. Deseret asked and was refused. Israel has a date, which makes it the exception in this essay rather than the template for it.

Ask instead who handed over which power, and every case answers at once. A letter assigns responsibility for an estate. A treaty protects merchants. A charter permits a company to raise troops. A revenue grant funds an army. Where these documents survive, they let us examine the terms on which authority was claimed or delegated. Some concern an estate or a revenue stream; others, such as Tordesillas, explicitly attempt to draw a boundary. Their subjects, procedures, and exclusions show what kind of authority each organization claimed—and what it still had to make effective.

## Why I went looking

I founded [ZaiGeZaiGu](https://zgzg.io), a Silicon Valley cultural nonprofit that began as a Lunar New Year gala, and I have started several other communities. Running them raises a question I could not answer from inside any of them. A mailing list becomes a club, a club becomes a nonprofit, and somewhere past that, apparently, is a state. I wanted to know what that last step consists of.

I am also fascinated by building [digital trust](/blog/d3serve-manifesto-digital-trust/), and by the social process through which [jurisdiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisdiction) takes shape. How does a community come to recognize someone's authority to make rules, resolve a dispute, or enforce a decision? Who accepts that authority, who contests it, and what makes it effective? Those questions connect my work on digital institutions to these historical cases.

One concrete effort is my proposal [ERC-5485: Jurisdiction, Accreditation, and Enforcement](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-5485). It seeks to standardize how [smart contracts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smart_contract) express the jurisdiction they observe, the authority that accredits them, and a way to receive enforcement proposals. It [distinguishes declaring allegiance from being recognized by an authority](#ref-erc5485). As of this revision, the proposal is in Review; it is an effort to model institutional relationships, rather than a claim that declaring a jurisdiction in code establishes legal authority.

What interests me is the gap between a declaration and a functioning relationship. A contract can name an authority; a community must still decide why that authority should be recognized and how its decisions will be treated. The interface explicitly permits implementations to [record, reject, or act on an enforcement proposal](#ref-erc5485). These histories give that technical distinction substance: claiming power, receiving recognition, and making a decision effective are different achievements.

The four questions New Harmony left vague are not abstract to me either. For ZaiGeZaiGu's 2018 singing competition a few of us built a peer-recognition system for volunteers, modeled on the one Google used internally. We redesigned it for the next year's gala and gave it a new name. Usage went up. Most volunteers still ignored it. That is one of the four questions, in one community, at the scale of a single event, and we did not solve it.

So I went looking for the ceiling of a community. What held my attention was the work of constructing jurisdiction: the grants, agreements, appointments, and enforcement arrangements through which people made authority real—and the conflicts over whether they had the right to do so.
