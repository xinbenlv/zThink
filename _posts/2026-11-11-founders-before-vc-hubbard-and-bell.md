In May 1877 a one-page circular went out under the name of Gardiner G. Hubbard, a Cambridge lawyer. It offered a new instrument that carried speech over a wire, and it did not offer to sell one. The terms were [$20 a year to lease two telephones for social use and $40 a year for business use](#ref-watson-circular), payable every six months in advance. The owners would keep the instruments in working order, and an extra telephone on the same line cost another $10 a year.

This series asks a narrow question: before modern venture capital, which the series dates from the founding of ARD in 1946, who worked most like today's startup founders? The telephone is an obvious candidate, but the famous version of the story gives most of the credit to the inventor's genius and a rival's blindness. The surviving papers point somewhere else. They show a business decision made early, written down in a price list, and defended against pressure to take quick cash.

## The telephone began as a side project of a telegraph venture

Bell did not start out with backers for the telephone. In February 1875 Bell, Hubbard and Thomas Sanders signed [a written agreement forming the "Bell Patent Association"](#ref-watson-chronology) to promote Bell's work in the telegraph field. Bell's main project then was a multiple telegraph that would send several messages over one wire. Thomas Watson, the machinist who built Bell's apparatus, later recalled that [Hubbard and Sanders, as Bell's financial backers](#ref-watson-backers), were insisting that Bell should finish the harmonic telegraph first and leave "air castles like the telephone" for later.

The incentive was clear. Telegraphy was the large, proven market, and Western Union dominated it. In 1879 Hubbard described his rival to his own stockholders as a company [with a capital of over $40,000,000](#ref-hubbard-1879-competition), "controlling the best electric talent in the country". A better telegraph had an obvious buyer. A device that carried the human voice had none yet.

Bell filed his telephone patent application on February 14, 1876. [U.S. Patent 174,465](#ref-patent) was issued on March 7, 1876, under the title "Improvement in Telegraphy". Its fifth claim covered "the method of, and apparatus for, transmitting vocal or other sounds telegraphically". On June 25, 1876, Bell [showed the telephone to the judges of the Centennial Exhibition in Philadelphia](#ref-watson-chronology-1876).

The partners were also linked by family. Hubbard was president of a Boston school for deaf children, and his daughter Mabel had been deaf since the age of five. Bell had met Hubbard [at that school in 1872 and later taught Mabel as a private pupil](#ref-wolff-hubbard-met). In 1877, the same year he formed the Bell Telephone Company, Bell [married Mabel Hubbard](#ref-loc-essay). Hubbard was therefore the inventor's partner, organizer and father-in-law at once.

Sanders put in most of the money. Herbert Casson's 1910 history says Sanders, whose business was [cutting out soles for shoe manufacturers](#ref-casson-sanders), advanced nine-tenths of the money spent on the telephone from 1874 to 1878. Michael Wolff, writing in *IEEE Spectrum* in 1976 and relying on Robert Bruce's biography of Bell, says that by March 1878 Sanders [had sunk almost his whole fortune of $110,000 into the venture](#ref-wolff-sanders). Both are secondary accounts, and the figures are theirs.

## Hubbard chose to rent the instrument instead of selling it

The circular of May 1877 is the clearest record of the business model. It argued that the telephone beat the telegraph for local business because [no skilled operator was required](#ref-watson-circular), speech was faster than Morse code, and the instrument needed no battery. Then it set out the lease terms quoted above and ended: "Parties leasing the Telephones incur no expense beyond the annual rental and the repair of the line wire." The same circular was [reprinted in *Popular Science Monthly* in 1906](#ref-psm-1906), so the text does not depend on a single later copy.

The choice was not obvious inside the family. Wolff writes that [Mrs. Hubbard seems to have pushed for the outright sale of telephones](#ref-wolff-lease), while her husband preferred leasing. Wolff quotes Watson's later memoir: "Some of the ladies deeply interested in the immediate outcome were strenuously advocating at this critical juncture making and selling the telephones at once in the largest possible quantities—imperfect as they were." Selling would have brought cash immediately to a group that badly needed it. Leasing meant the company paid for the instruments now and collected rent slowly.

In my reading, the lease kept three things with the patent holders. First, the instrument stayed company property, so a customer could not resell it. Second, the company could replace instruments as the technology improved. Third, rent came every year. Hubbard's 1879 report to the stockholders, a manuscript in the Library of Congress, shows where this led. By then the company had [about 12,000 telephones outstanding at a net rental of about $5.00 a year](#ref-hubbard-1879-rental), and Hubbard expected the average net rental to rise. This article checked that sentence against the manuscript image, not only against the transcription.

Hubbard also used other people's capital to spread the network. He gave local agents exclusive territories. Watson recalls that Fred Gower held [an exclusive right Mr. Hubbard had granted him for renting telephones all over New England](#ref-watson-gower), and later traded it away for lecture rights. By 1879 the growth came through "district companies", local firms that charged customers a monthly fee for service. Hubbard's report describes agents who [were stimulated by a large commission](#ref-hubbard-1879-agents) and who made the telephone their main business. Hubbard explained why speed mattered: ["the Co. that first occupies the field in any city must hold it against any new comer"](#ref-hubbard-1879-competition).

Western Union entered the telephone business with a transmitter developed by Edison, and the Bell company [sued Western Union for infringing the Bell patents](#ref-watson-toy). On November 10, 1879, the two companies signed an agreement under which Western Union [admitted the validity of the Bell patents and retired from the telephone field](#ref-wolff-1879). In return, the Bell company agreed to pay Western Union 20 percent of its royalties from telephone rentals for 17 years. By the end of 1880 there were [47,900 Bell telephones in the United States](#ref-watson-chronology-1880), according to the chronology that AT&T printed with Watson's address.

## Today's founders would call it hardware as a service with franchised distribution

In today's terms, the model has three parts. This section is my interpretation, not a claim from the sources.

1. **Recurring revenue instead of one-time sales.** The 1877 circular is a subscription price list. The customer paid for working service, maintenance included, and the company kept ownership of the hardware. Today we call this hardware as a service.
2. **Control of the installed base.** Because every instrument stayed company property, the company kept a relationship with every user. A buyer of an outright telephone could have resold it. A renter could not.
3. **Distribution paid for by partners.** Exclusive local agents and district companies built the lines and found customers with their own money in return for a commission. That is close to a franchise or a channel-partner program. Hubbard's "first occupies the field" sentence is an early statement of land-grab thinking: the first network in a city tends to keep it.

What Hubbard did not have was outside risk capital. The money came from Sanders, from Hubbard's own family, and from agents who invested in their own territories. That is the main reason this story belongs in a series about founders before venture capital. The financing was personal, and the business model had to fund its own growth.

## Several famous stories about Western Union do not survive checking

### The Western Union "toy" memo is a later fabrication

A memo often quoted in business books has a Western Union committee reject the telephone: "This idea is idiotic on the face of it," and the device is "hardly more than a toy". Wolff printed the text in 1976 and wrote that [the report "is suspect on several counts"](#ref-wolff-memo). It is addressed to Chauncey Depew as president of Western Union, but Depew was never its president and joined its board only in 1881. It is unsigned and apparently not on company stationery. A 2015 article on the comic site *Wondermark* reports that the text first appeared in a 1968 IEEE publication by Richard C. Levine, who later told the historian Phil Lapsley that [he had put it together from two similar but not identical versions](#ref-wondermark). That account of Levine's letter is secondhand, and I could not open Lapsley's original blog posts. The conclusion does not depend on it: no source this article found shows the memo existing in 1876.

### The $100,000 offer rests mainly on Bell's side of the story

The story that Hubbard offered all the Bell patents to Western Union for $100,000 has a specific source. In an address delivered on October 17, 1913, Watson said the president of Western Union had [refused, somewhat contemptuously, Mr. Hubbard's offer](#ref-watson-100k) to sell all the Bell patents for "the exorbitant sum of $100,000". Watson added that two years later Western Union would gladly have paid $25,000,000. That figure is Watson's claim, not a documented offer.

Wolff searched for other evidence in 1976 and found [almost none](#ref-wolff-100k). Bruce placed the offer in late 1876 or the winter of 1876–77 but found no mention of it in Bell's private correspondence. Two scholars who examined Western Union's "President's Letter Books" for that period found no mention either, and none has been found in Hubbard's surviving letters to Bell. Wolff concluded that the only evidence it even occurred was Watson's 1913 recollection. Casson's 1910 history supplies the famous line in which Orton asks, "What use could this company make of [an electrical toy](#ref-casson-toy)?", but Casson gives no source for the quotation.

A third account points in a different direction. In his 1924 memoir, Chauncey Depew wrote that in 1876 Hubbard [offered him a one-sixth interest for ten thousand dollars](#ref-depew). Depew says he asked William Orton, the actual president of Western Union, who told him there was nothing in the scheme "except as a toy". Depew dropped the investment. This is a recollection written almost fifty years later, and it has its own errors: Depew quotes Hubbard calling Bell "my son-in-law" in 1876, although the marriage took place in 1877.

So the strongest honest statement is this: Bell's associates later remembered an offer, and Depew remembered a smaller one. No contemporary document found so far confirms the $100,000 figure.

### Orton's personal dislike of Hubbard is also weaker than the legend

Popular versions often explain the refusal by saying [Orton hated Hubbard](#ref-wondermark), who had opposed Western Union in Congress and in the courts. The historian David Hochfelder traced that explanation to [two ultimate sources](#ref-hochfelder-orton), a Watson letter of December 1877 and a claim by George Prescott, and noted that Hubbard's papers show the two men [on friendly terms](#ref-hochfelder-orton), even dining together. Hochfelder also cites a January 1878 letter in which Hubbard himself [doubted "if any instrument of value can be made under [Bell's] patents of 1876"](#ref-hochfelder-1878). If the patent holder had doubts about the 1876 patents, a cautious telegraph company may have had reasons other than blindness.

### The shoe-machinery inspiration for leasing is not established

A common modern claim says Hubbard copied the lease model from Gordon McKay's shoe-sewing machines, which were leased to factories for royalties. The English Wikipedia article on McKay says the legal arrangements were [partially handled by Gardiner Greene Hubbard](#ref-mckay-wikipedia), citing a 1926 book, *Pioneering the Telephone in Canada*, at page 17. I could not open that book, and I found no document in which Hubbard or his partners name McKay as the model for telephone leasing. The parallel is real: both businesses leased patented machines. The causal link is unverified, so this article does not rely on it.

## The lesson for founders is to decide early what the customer actually buys

This section is my interpretation.

Hubbard's 1877 circular sold a working service, not a box. That choice decided how the company made money, who owned the hardware, and how fast outside agents could expand the network. It was made when the product was crude, cash was scarce, and, by Wolff's account, a member of the founder's family wanted to sell instruments immediately.

For a founder building developer tools, the question is the same: is the customer buying a binary, a hosted service, or a guarantee that something keeps working? For a business built on domain names, the question is whether customers want to own a name outright or want a name that stays configured, renewed and protected. In both cases the answer sets the pricing, the support burden, and the defensibility of the business.

The other lesson concerns the stories founders repeat. The "Western Union said it was a toy" anecdote is useful because it flatters the outsider. Its documentary basis is a forged memo, a 1913 recollection by an interested party, an unsourced line in a 1910 popular history, and a 1924 memoir. The record that survives in Hubbard's own hand is less dramatic and more useful: a price list, a commission structure, and a plan to occupy each city first.
